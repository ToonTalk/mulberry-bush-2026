Twelve times round the mulberry bush

A hand-made dance film from 1976, and the eleven remakes that six AI systems made of it this month. Reviewed in order of how far each one travels from the original.

Reviewed by Claude Fable 5.2, writing as a critic. How the films were watched is at the end.

The 1976 film asks one question for three minutes: how many ways can you draw someone dancing? Four people skip across a pink screen as paint, as shadow, as stripes, as dots, as coloured discs. The drawing keeps changing and the dance never does.

Fifty years on, the filmmaker gave it to six AI systems from three companies and asked for remakes, some faithful and some radical. The eleven results sit on ten web pages, since one page holds two. They fall into four kinds. Some redraw the film. Some put it back inside the evening it was made for, when four dancers circled a backlit screen before and after the projection. One prints from it. One leaves it behind.

I have judged them by the standard the original sets. Its subject is the dancing body and its method is the hand. A remake is free to drop either, as long as it puts something of equal interest in the gap.

The original

Mulberry Bush

Ken Kahn, 19762:5416mm, hand-drawnWatch

Four dancers cross a rose-pink field, often hand in hand. Every few seconds the drawing changes: flat paint, often without faces; black cut-outs; grey wash; horizontal stripes with scribbled hair; figures pricked out in dots; bodies built from red, blue and yellow discs. Two styles often share the frame, so a painted woman holds the hand of a silhouette. The figures pass close and then far, as if circling the camera, and the screen is left empty between passes.

The movement is the film’s strength. It is evidently traced from filmed dancers, so it has weight: knees give, a skirt swings late, an arm overreaches. The marks are quick and unfussy, and the dots and discs are funnier than they have any right to be. At 1:53 the dots give up being a dancer and slide into a heap in the corner.

Its limit is that it permutes and does not build. The styles are shuffled, nothing accumulates, and the film stops when the list runs out. As the middle panel of a live piece that was probably enough. Alone, it is a charming study with one very good idea.

This is an edition more than a remake. Every drawing of the original has been traced to outlines and is redrawn in the browser at twelve drawings a second, inside a rounded film gate with grain. It keeps the original’s cutting and its empty frames, so most of its virtues are borrowed.

What it adds is a row of buttons: redraw the dancers as shadows, stripes, dots, big circles or polka dots, at any moment. That is a sharper idea than it looks. The 1976 film shuffles its styles. Here the viewer does the shuffling, and a passage of paint becomes a passage of stripes with the same knees and elbows underneath. The film’s premise becomes an instrument. A legend also assigns each style a player in a new band: fiddle for paint, banjo for stripes, tuba and drum for the discs.

The cost is on the surface. Tracing turns the paint into posterised digital blotches and takes some of the hand’s wobble with it, and the film gate and grain are costume. Open it after the original, for the buttons.

The most studious remake here. Its making-of describes sampling the 1976 film four times a second and logging which kind of drawing was on screen at each moment. The film plays that log back with figures of its own. On cue they are painted, cut out, striped, dotted or built from stickers. They vanish where the original cuts to empty paper, and at 1:53 the dots slide into a heap in the corner, as they do in 1976. The pink ground is the film’s own paper, recovered from its blank frames, and the credits are the original hand-lettered cards.

It has looked at the original harder than any other entry. It caught the pauses on empty paper, the giant who crosses the foreground, and the way the striped men are hatched across and the striped women down. I had missed the heap of dots until this film pointed at it. Its own hatching, with a ring of loops for hair and zigzag arms, is the one passage where its drawing has some wit.

It also puts itself beside its source, and that view sinks it. On the left two painted dancers fill the frame in mid-skip, hand in hand. On the right four small mannequins file past in a row with their arms out, like a paper chain. They are one jointed rig of blocks and trapezoids, and each style is a coat of paint on it. The log slips, too: at 1:32 the original is in stripes and the remake is in stickers.

The picture is a small square in a control desk: four views, three soundtracks, six stackable layers, five effects, three stamps for adding your own marks, four paper stocks and thirteen scene buttons. The page calls its canvas hand-made. It is drawn by code that jitters each outline twelve times a second. The real achievement is the notes. This is the best account of what happens in the 1976 film, with a puppet show to illustrate it.

Eight jointed paper figures dance on a rose stage, in a ring or in a line, while the viewer changes their costume: silhouettes, stripes, dots or colour. The page invites you to drag a dancer out of the circle and let it find its way home. The page itself is handsomely made, and the stripes costume, with its loose hatching and inked heads, is the one place where the figures come near the original’s line.

As animation it is thin. The eight are four copies of one man and one woman. Their limbs swing on pivots in a short cycle, and nothing happens in the third minute that did not happen in the first ten seconds. The costumes are skins on a single rig, so changing them changes nothing about how the bodies move. In the 1976 film each new mark made you see the movement afresh. Here the marks are wallpaper.

A pleasant toy. The second tab plays the original film with a new score, and that is the better use of three minutes.

Afterimage rebuilds the 1976 evening as a triptych. For forty seconds four jointed shadows circle a lit screen. Then the screen shows the original film, complete, for nearly three minutes. Then four pale figures return for fifty seconds, and a bush branches up between them and fruits.

The structure is right, and the proportions give the piece away. Two thirds of the running time is the 1976 footage, played straight with a few faint line figures laid over it. The new work amounts to two bookends, and the bookend dancers are mannequins: arms out, knees bending on a hinge, drifting round an ellipse like figures on a mobile. Beside the traced bodies in the middle they look inert, and every cut invites the comparison. Moving the pointer moves the light, which changes little.

The growing bush is the one image that belongs to this piece alone. It arrives with fifty seconds left.

Two films share one control room. The first is labelled “1976 Archive Film (Hand-Made 16mm)” and is nothing of the kind. It is a new animation in which pictogram people, circle heads on trapezoid skirts, skip across a pink film gate in the original’s styles. Viewers should be told plainly that this is not the archive film.

The second, “Radical Expanded Cinema”, is a three-act stage in real 3D. There is a backlit sheet, a lamp on the floor, the same pictogram dancers and a bundle of twigs for a bush. Then the film appears on the tilted sheet while sticker figures and ghost trails crowd in, and at the end everyone dances at once under the credits. You can orbit the camera, look down from above or stand behind the sheet. Sliders set the tempo, the height of the leaps, the frame rate, the wobble of the line and the mix of fiddle, banjo, concertina and projector whir.

Standing behind the sheet is a good idea, and it is buried. Every decision a filmmaker would make has been handed to the viewer as a control, and with the controls at their defaults the picture is a warm murk of overlapping glows. It is a generous entry and the hardest to watch.

The 1976 evening rebuilt as a working model. In the first act four dancers, each with a name tag floating over its head, circle a translucent pink sheet with a lamp behind it, and their shadows fall on the sheet. In the second the sheet becomes a cinema screen and shows six “worlds” of about fifteen seconds each, most of them grown from one of the original’s ways of drawing. Shadows split into cyan, magenta and yellow. Scanlines buckle where a body stands. Stick figures bud berries beside a tree. Clouds of dots spiral into a knot. Skeletons are strung with shaded balls. Bodies smear into rainbow trails. In the third act the dancers come back and all of it plays at once.

The first act is the reason to open it. The making-of proposes that the giants who sweep across the 1976 film are what a shadow does when its owner steps close to the lamp, and here you can watch it happen. A shadow swells to about twice the size of its neighbours as the ring turns. An overhead camera lays out the lamp, the sheet and the ring like a diagram. No other remake tries to explain the original’s pictures by its staging. Of the six worlds the scanlines are the best. The dancers exist only as a swell in the lines, which makes them true descendants of the 1976 stripes.

But it is a demonstration and it looks like one. The dancers are bars and discs with their arms held straight out. The lamp and the projector carry labels inside the picture, and the colours are the glowing cyan and magenta of a graphics demo. Six worlds in ninety seconds repeat the original’s weakness, which is to list its styles and not build, and none lasts long enough to become a dance. The names promise more than the screen delivers: the “Singularity Supernova” is two seconds of dots going down a drain. The finale layers everything at once and is hard to read.

It is close kin to Gemini’s three-act stage, reviewed above, and both come from Google: the same backlit sheet, lamp, orbiting camera and pictogram dancers, and the same phrase, “expanded cinema”. This one is easier to watch because its default camera knows where to look. It has five cameras, sliders for the lamp and the sheet, and a button that projects the 1976 film onto the little screen. Its first act taught me something about the original. The rest is a tour of effects.

A puppet film in three movements, and the one remake that goes back to the nursery rhyme, whose later verses are about washing clothes. Four jointed wooden dancers circle a bedsheet pegged to a line, throwing soft shadows on it. They leave. A spot of berry juice on the cloth spreads, the camera moves into the sheet, and the stain grows into a bush that leafs and fruits. The dancers come back carrying the fruit.

The sheet as the screen and the stain as the film is the neatest single conceit in the programme, and the film tells it clearly in under a minute and a half.

The telling is the problem. The drawing is flat, clean vector illustration of the kind used to explain things. The puppets move like diagrams of puppets, and the bush grows on rails. Nothing in it looks touched by a hand, which is a strange result for a film descended from stripes and dots made with one. The fruit, pink and clustered, reads as raspberries.

The best idea in the programme. Shadow dancers circle a disc of pink paper with a small bush at its centre and the words of the rhyme round its rim. Two musicians sit in silhouette in the front row. Then the paper turns out to be a phenakistoscope, the slotted spinning disc that animated drawings sixty years before cinema. Its two rings fill with dancers holding hands, first as shadows, then in paint, dots and discs. At one point the wheel smears into a blur, as it would without its slots, and for a second you see what the device is made of. The shadows return at the end and dance in front of it.

It answers the 1976 evening exactly: live bodies, then a machine for drawing bodies, then bodies again. It also earns its interaction. You can take the wheel and turn it by hand, and choose a style for each ring, which is what such a wheel was for.

The drawing lets it down a little. The figures on the wheel are identical paper dolls in one symmetrical pose, so a ring of them makes a pattern before it makes a dance, and the pleasure is nearer a kaleidoscope’s than that of watching anyone move. The rose-pink stays close to home. But the form is thought through, from the rhyme to the mechanism.

The shortest film here and the most assured as image-making. Roundelay lifts outlines from the original and prints them twice on cream paper, once in blue-green and once in vermilion, slightly apart in space and in time. Where the two inks overlap, the figure goes black. A hand reaches out in red where the blue hand was a moment before. Norman McLaren multiplied two ballet dancers this way with an optical printer in Pas de deux. Doing it as a misregistered two-colour print is a real find, and it suits drawings that were about marks in the first place.

It has a shape, too. Four passages of twenty seconds take it from two offset figures, through stripes and dots, to four groups set round an ellipse, and finally to a chain of dancers whose two impressions slide into register and hold.

Its weaknesses are the borrowed gesture, since every pose comes from 1976, and the mulberry. A neat cluster of red fruit sits in each shot like a logo and never joins the printing. At eighty seconds the film is slight. It is also the only one of the four ChatGPT films I wanted to see twice.

The only remake that throws the figures out. The camera looks straight down on dark ground. Four lanterns, rose, teal, violet and gold, walk in from the corners and circle a seed. A bush grows toward them in a pinwheel, leafs, fruits, and is stripped by a whirl of yellow leaves. The dancers are never drawn. They are four points of light and their footprints, pale at first and purple once they have run through the fallen fruit. The night ends with one long shadow at sunrise and the rhyme written in a ring.

This is the most complete film of the eleven. It has a beginning, a climax and an ending, and its picture is consistently beautiful: soft pools of mixed colour, grain, the dawn. The inversion is real thinking too. In 1976 the light was behind the dancers, and here the dancers are the light.

What it gives up is the thing the original was about. Points of light do not have knees. The choreography is circling, one weave, a run and a stop. At normal size the footprints read as dashes, so the body survives mainly as an idea. The single overhead view never changes, the first minute is dim and slow to declare itself, and the turn of the seasons is a little obvious. It is a lovely film about a bush. I am less sure it is a film about dancing.

In sum

What to watch

If you watch three, watch the 1976 film, Round the Mulberry Bush and Mulberry Bush (2026), in that order, with Roundelay as a coda. The first has the bodies. The second has the best form. The third has the best pictures. The fourth has the best eighty seconds of printmaking. If you would rather study the original than watch a remake of it, open the side-by-side view of the Interactive Hand-Made Edition and the first act of the Radical 3-Act Reinterpretation.

Seen together, the remakes share a habit. Nearly all of them understood the film as a structure, whether a set of styles or an evening in three parts, and rebuilt that structure with care. Almost none of them could draw a person dancing. They offer jointed puppets, pictograms, paper dolls and points of light. The Interactive Hand-Made Edition is the clearest case: it timed the original to the quarter second and filled it with mannequins. The four dancers of 1976 were traced from life by hand, and fifty years on they are still the only ones in the room who look as if they have weight.

How these were watched. I am an AI model, Claude Fable 5.2, writing in the role of a critic at the filmmaker’s request. Two of the films were made by the same model as me and one by a related model. The two Antigravity pages do not say which model did the work, so they are credited to the tool. I was asked to set that aside, along with the filmmaker’s own published opinions, and I have tried to.

I watched without sound. I saw the 1976 film as one frame every two seconds, and sampled each remake at six to twenty points across its length instead of playing it through in real time. I operated the controls of the interactive ones. So these reviews judge picture, movement, structure and interaction, and say nothing about how any soundtrack sounds. Where an instrument is mentioned, it is named on the film’s own page.
